## Epic Summary

Epic 4 delivered the playback half of foundational tuplet support. The team completed four story slices covering exact host-time projection, deterministic mixed-passage ordering, explicit preservation of legacy non-tuplet playback behavior, and confirmation that MVP playback remains scoped to the active flow.

The defining outcome of the epic was not merely that playback worked. It was that playback was added without major architectural churn because the underlying model stayed consistent across audio, notation, persistence, and sequencing. That consistency reduced story churn, avoided repeated semantic rediscovery, and made ergonomic iteration cheap.

## What Went Well

- The shared tuplet model held across subsystems. Playback consumed the same semantic contract already established by persistence, notation, rendering, and sequencing instead of creating a transport-only interpretation.
- Story execution stayed smooth because the team did not need to repeatedly refine stories after failed attempts. Stable rules meant each story could extend the contract rather than renegotiate it.
- Ergonomic improvements surfaced naturally through MVP use and were incorporated as local refinements. The team was able to iterate on real usage feedback without destabilizing architecture.
- The story sequence stayed disciplined: projection first, then mixed ordering, then legacy host-only protection, then active-flow scope proof.
- Validation stayed credible and focused. The story records consistently cite targeted Rust regressions plus full `cargo test --manifest-path src-tauri/Cargo.toml` and `cargo clippy --manifest-path src-tauri/Cargo.toml --all-targets --all-features` runs.
- Scope control remained strong. The team delivered active-flow tuplet playback without quietly expanding MVP into cross-flow playback or broader transport UX changes.

## Challenges And Friction

### 1. The main friction was process overhead, not architecture

Epic 4 did not surface major implementation instability. The friction that remained was mostly artifact overhead: repeated guardrail language across closely related story records and regression evidence distributed across story-local completion notes.

### 2. Successful execution can hide why it was successful

Because the epic felt smooth, there is a risk that future contributors will see only the final code and miss the discipline that made the work smooth. The real win was semantic consistency across subsystems, not just competent local implementation.

### 3. Administrative status lag remains visible

The story records show Epic 4 is complete from a story perspective, but [implementation-artifacts/sprint-status.yaml](_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/sprint-status.yaml) still lists `epic-4` as `in-progress`. That mismatch did not block the work, but it is a small maintenance smell in project tracking.

## Key Learnings

- The biggest reason Epic 4 went smoothly was semantic consistency across audio, notation, persistence, and sequencing.
- Stable models reduce both implementation churn and story churn. When the rules stay coherent, teams do not need to repeatedly remind themselves of the contract.
- Brownfield work feels smooth when local refinements happen inside a stable model instead of compensating for a weak one.
- Playback can be added cleanly to a notation-heavy system when it consumes shared semantics instead of inventing its own rhythm model.
- MVP usage is an effective source of ergonomic feedback when the underlying architecture is already trustworthy.

## Evidence From The Epic
