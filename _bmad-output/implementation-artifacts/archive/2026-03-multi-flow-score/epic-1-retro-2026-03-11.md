## Overall Outcome

Epic 1 is a strong start to the multi-render work stream. The product now renders all visible flows on one continuous shared score surface in flow order, and the implementation validated the architectural direction without requiring drastic rewrites. The codebase feels understandable, maintainable, and extendable, with the render planner/orchestration seam emerging as a useful long-term flexibility point.

Epic 1 is accepted, integrated into the active project baseline, and has no identified blockers preventing Epic 2.

## What Went Well

- Testing and validation created real trust in AI-assisted implementation work.
- Manual testing remained useful and complemented the growing automated test base.
- The team extended existing foundations instead of rewriting major subsystems.
- The shared-surface render path was reused incrementally across the epic rather than spawning competing presentation models.
- Notation behavior, persistence semantics, and backend/frontend ownership boundaries remained intact.
- The codebase remains understandable even to a reader who did not author the implementation.
- Epic 1 likely retired enough foundational risk that Epic 2 may be lighter in implementation scope than originally expected.

## Challenges And Friction

### 1. Ambiguous preservation wording

The intent behind wording such as preserving single-flow behavior was interpreted too literally as preserving a separate single-flow implementation path. That led to temporary architectural drag and a later cleanup story. The process handled the correction well, but the wording should have been sharper.

### 2. Rejected flow-label direction

The passive flow-label idea looked plausible in planning but performed poorly in the real UI. The change added confusion rather than clarity and was correctly reverted. This was a useful signal that UX-facing ideas need earlier live validation when their value is hard to judge from the artifact alone.

### 3. Diagnostic truthfulness matters

Several follow-ups during the epic were not about whether a feature existed, but whether the debugger and supporting evidence told the truth clearly enough for maintainers to trust what they were seeing.

## Key Learnings

- Preservation requirements should default to preserving behavior unless interface or implementation preservation is explicitly intended.
- UX-sensitive stories should call out live validation as part of done when artifact-only reasoning is insufficient.
- Temporary implementation compromises are acceptable only if they are named honestly and have an explicit cleanup condition.
- Fast rollback is a strength. Reverting a weak idea is better than defending sunk cost.
- Validation should remain strict even when a later epic looks lighter in development effort.

## Epic 2 Carry-Forwards

- Epic 2 may be a light development epic because Epic 1 already delivered much of the shared-surface groundwork.
- Validation must remain strict because the remaining work is correctness-sensitive: hit-testing, selection stability, popover anchoring, and active-flow clarity.
- Epic 2 should start with explicit wording around behavior preservation versus implementation preservation.
- Epic 2 should avoid reviving passive page labels as an orientation default.

## Action Items

### Process Improvements

1. Add a story-writing rule that preservation requirements must specify whether they preserve behavior, interface contract, or implementation structure.
   - Owner: Bob (Scrum Master)
   - Success criteria: Future architecture-sensitive stories use explicit wording such as "preserve single-flow behavior" rather than ambiguous preservation language.

2. Mark UX-sensitive stories as requiring live validation whenever the value cannot be trusted from the written artifact alone.
   - Owner: John (Product Manager)
   - Success criteria: Future UX-facing stories call out live product review as part of done.

3. Call out temporary implementation compromises explicitly, with cleanup intent captured at story time.
   - Owner: Winston (Architect)
   - Success criteria: Temporary branches or compatibility paths are labeled temporary with a stated removal condition.

4. Maintain the current validation bar for AI-assisted implementation work.
   - Owners: Quinn (QA Engineer), Amelia (Developer)
   - Success criteria: Story completion continues to require meaningful validation evidence rather than narrative claims.

### Technical And Delivery Hygiene

5. Treat debugger and diagnostic truthfulness as part of feature completeness whenever verification tooling is part of acceptance.
   - Owner: Quinn (QA Engineer)
   - Priority: High
   - Success criteria: Stories that rely on diagnostics also prove those diagnostics are accurate and not stale or misleading.

6. Add an explicit review check for whether a story preserved behavior or preserved legacy code.
   - Owner: Bob (Scrum Master)
   - Success criteria: Architecture-sensitive reviews explicitly ask that question before closure.
