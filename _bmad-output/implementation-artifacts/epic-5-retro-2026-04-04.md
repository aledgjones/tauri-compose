## Epic Summary And Assessment

- Completed stories: 7/7
- Epic 5 delivered the intended product outcome: transport-driven score playback became trustworthy enough to support real composition work across play, pause, resume, seek, bounded editing, reopen, and degraded-route recovery.
- The epic stayed disciplined around backend ownership: transport remained backend-owned, the continuous engine remained the realtime playback seam, and the frontend remained a thin trigger and display layer.
- The largest process failure was not implementation quality. It was story dependency modeling.

## What Went Well

- The final delivered architecture remained coherent across the full epic. Transport ownership, engine ownership, runtime readiness, and persistence boundaries stayed aligned with the approved backend-owned playback model.
- The team corrected course without diluting the product path. Once the missing foundation was recognized, the epic inserted the needed baseline story and re-anchored the later stories to that corrected foundation instead of layering more assumptions on top of it.
- Playback trust improved in the right order once the correction happened: audible transport baseline, transport reset hardening, seek correctness, clocking and reset hardening, bounded live editing, reopen integrity, and graceful degraded-route recovery.
- The score-first and route-first product surface stayed compact. Epic 5 did not sprawl into proof UI, host-debug workflows, or fallback-driven behavior.
- Validation stayed close to the owning seams. The epic consistently reinforced backend regression coverage around transport, engine, preparation, runtime activation, and file/load behavior.

## Main Failure

Epic 5 contained a major story-sequencing defect.

- Foundational score playback was implied by downstream trust stories instead of being explicitly established in the original story progression.
- Higher-level planning was directionally correct: the PRD and architecture already treated real transport-driven routed playback as foundational.
- The failure happened when that intent was decomposed into story order. Story-level dependencies were not modeled explicitly enough for safe execution.
- This was not a minor wording issue. It caused real sequencing damage in an architecture-sensitive epic.

## Evidence Of The Failure

- Story 5.1 was originally created and implemented as transport trust-hardening on top of an assumed already-audible routed transport path.
- The missing prerequisite had to be corrected by inserting Story 5.0 so Epic 5 explicitly owned making transport drive real routed score playback.
- Story 5.2 was later rewritten against the corrected baseline rather than continuing unchanged from the original sequence.
- Story 5.2a was added as a fill-in hardening story to cover playback clocking and runtime reset semantics that the original progression had not carved out clearly enough.
- This proves the original story inventory did not encode the real dependency chain explicitly enough.

## What Was Hard

- The main challenge was not discovering the correct playback architecture. The challenge was turning that architecture into an explicit, safe story progression.
- Epic 5 transport work remained highly sensitive to teardown, reset, queue, and restart semantics. Reviews repeatedly found real edge failures around stale queued work, pause and seek rebasing, shared-slot cleanup, and rebuild timing assumptions.
- Because the original progression hid the foundational dependency, correct local implementation could still happen in the wrong order.

### Continuity Insights

- Epic 4's architectural ownership lessons were largely preserved in implementation.
- Epic 4's process lesson about earlier architectural alignment was only partially absorbed. The team had the right higher-level understanding but still let a key dependency remain implicit at the story level.
- The main carry-forward improvement is not a new playback seam. It is stronger story decomposition discipline for architecture-sensitive work.

## Key Lessons Learned

1. If a runtime capability is foundational in the PRD or architecture, the story sequence must name it explicitly before downstream trust, UX, or realism stories begin.
2. A story can be directionally correct and still be wrongly sequenced if the inventory leaves a prerequisite implicit.
3. Architecture-sensitive epics need explicit distinction between foundation stories, extension stories, and hardening stories.
4. Playback trust is won at reset, teardown, and restart boundaries. Even after the sequencing correction, these remained the highest-risk technical seams.
5. Strong backend ownership and seam-local validation let the team recover from planning defects without collapsing into parallel models or UI-heavy compensation.

## Significant Discoveries

- The most important discovery was process, not product: Epic 5's higher-level intent was viable, but the story progression did not make the audible transport foundation explicit soon enough.
- Once corrected, the epic showed that the existing backend model could support composition-grade playback trust without abandoning the score-first product surface.
- The fill-in Story 5.2a confirmed that platform hardening work sometimes needs its own explicit story rather than being absorbed invisibly into user-facing transport stories.
